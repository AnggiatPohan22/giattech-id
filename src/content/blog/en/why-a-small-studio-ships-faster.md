Clients often assume a bigger team means faster delivery. On web projects, that relationship frequently inverts. Not because people at large agencies are less capable — many are highly capable — but because structure creates costs that never appear on a quote.

This article explains those costs, and the honest limitations of a small studio like ours.

## Coordination cost grows faster than headcount

Two people have one communication path. Five have ten. Ten have forty-five.

Each path is a place information can be lost or altered. On web projects, the lost information is usually a small detail that turns out to be decisive: a business rule mentioned once in the second meeting, or a priority change that never reached the developer.

## The distance between the client and the builder

This is the most noticeable difference.

At a large agency the chain typically runs: client → account manager → project manager → tech lead → developer. Four hops, and each hop is a chance for interpretation to drift.

At a small studio, the person who hears your requirement is often the person writing the code. Context isn't lost because it was never handed off.

The practical effect: a question that takes two days to answer in one setup gets answered in one message in the other.

## Fewer decisions, made faster

A web project moves at the speed of its decisions. If every small change needs three layers of approval, the project moves at the pace of the slowest layer.

A small studio can decide small things in minutes. That sounds trivial until you count how many small decisions a project contains — usually hundreds.

## Clear ownership

When one person owns a piece end to end, there's no gap where "I thought the other team was handling that" lives. The work that most often falls into that gap is the work that hurts most to lose: testing on real devices, error handling, and responsive details at unusual screen sizes.

## The honest limitations of a small studio

This section matters, because a small studio isn't the answer to every case.

**Limited capacity.** We can't run five large projects simultaneously. If your deadline is tight and the scope is large, a bigger team is the correct answer.

**Concentration risk.** If one key person is ill, it's felt. Large agencies have bench depth.

**Depth of specialisation.** We don't have a dedicated user researcher, a privacy lawyer, or an internal security team. For projects that need those, we say so up front.

**Enterprise scale.** Projects spanning multiple departments, formal procurement, and regulatory compliance are better served by an organisation built for it.

## When to pick a small studio

- The project is well-defined and you want direct communication
- You value iteration speed over formal process
- The budget needs to go into building, not into a management layer
- You want to know exactly who is working on your project

## When to pick a large agency

- Scope is very large and the deadline is tight
- You need several disciplines at once (research, branding, campaign, development)
- Your organisation needs formal procurement and contractual guarantees
- You need long-term guarantees of team continuity

## What to ask both

Regardless of size, these questions open a lot up:

1. Who exactly will write the code?
2. How many other projects are they running at the same time?
3. If I have a technical question, how long until the right person answers?
4. Who is still available after launch?

The answers tell you more than the company size does. The full checklist is in [choosing a web development vendor](/blog/en/checklist-for-choosing-a-web-development-vendor/).

## How a small studio covers its weaknesses

We don't pretend the limitations above don't exist. What we do:

- **Enough documentation** that someone else could continue the project
- **Readable code**, not clever code
- **Repository and access in the client's name** from day one
- **Honesty about capacity.** If we can't meet your deadline, we say so before the contract, not in week six.

## Closing

Team size doesn't determine quality. What determines it is the distance between the person who understands the problem and the person who solves it. A small studio has a structural advantage there — and structural disadvantages elsewhere.

What matters is choosing deliberately rather than assuming bigger is always safer.
