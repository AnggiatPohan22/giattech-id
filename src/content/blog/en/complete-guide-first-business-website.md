Plenty of business owners put off building a website for one reason: they don't know what the first step is. The sequence is actually fairly standard. This guide lays it out as it really runs — from the business decisions up front to the moment Google can index the site.

It's written for you if you've never had a website, or you've had one that never produced anything.

## Step 1 — Commit to one primary goal

The most expensive mistake happens before a single line of code exists: the site gets built without a clear goal, and then everyone on the team judges the result by a different yardstick.

Pick **one** primary goal:

- **Generate leads** — visitors submit a form or start a WhatsApp chat.
- **Sell directly** — visitors complete a transaction on the site.
- **Build credibility** — prospects check you out before signing a contract.
- **Serve existing customers** — rebooking, status checks, document downloads.

Other goals can exist, but they're secondary. This one decides which pages get built, which button gets the loudest colour, and which number you watch every month.

> If you can't name a single number that should go up because of this website, the project isn't ready to start.

## Step 2 — Plan the page structure before you think about design

Structure is the skeleton. Design is the skin. Building the skeleton first saves an enormous amount of revision time.

For a service business, the minimum structure that actually works:

1. **Home** — what you do, who it's for, and what happens next.
2. **Services** — one page per major service if you're serious about SEO.
3. **Portfolio / case studies** — proof, not claims.
4. **About** — who is behind the business.
5. **Contact** — one page, one form, one number.
6. **Blog** — your long-term SEO engine.

For products or retail, add a catalogue page, product detail pages, and a checkout flow.

### The three-second rule

Visitors decide whether to stay almost instantly. The top of your homepage has to answer three things at once: **what you sell, who it's for, and what to click**. If any of those requires scrolling to find, you lose most of your traffic.

## Step 3 — Domain and hosting

**The domain** is your address. Pick something short, easy to spell over the phone, and take `.com` if it's available. Country domains like `.id` or `.co.id` are strong for the Indonesian market, but `.co.id` requires company documents.

Some practical rules:

- Avoid hyphens and digits — they're painful to dictate.
- Grab common misspellings if the budget allows.
- Register it under **your company's name**, never your vendor's. This matters more than people realise.

**Hosting** determines how fast your site opens. For a static or small business site, good shared hosting is enough. For a web application with a database and user logins, you need a VPS or a managed platform. We cover the trade-offs in [choosing hosting for a business website](/blog/en/choosing-hosting-for-a-business-website/).

## Step 4 — Choose how to build it

There are three routes, and none of them is wrong. What's wrong is picking a route that doesn't match the goal from Step 1.

### Website builders (Wix, Squarespace, Webflow)

Fast, cheap up front, great for validating an idea. The limits appear when you need custom integrations or maximum performance.

### A CMS (WordPress and friends)

The largest plugin ecosystem in the world and plenty of people who can operate it. The trade-offs are plugin weight, security exposure, and maintenance costs that never stop.

### A custom build

Built to the requirement. Fastest to load, most flexible, and the sensible choice when the website is a core part of how the business runs. Full comparison in [Astro vs WordPress](/blog/en/astro-vs-wordpress-which-one-fits/).

## Step 5 — Get the content ready before the design is done

This is the step people invert most often, and it's the number one reason website projects slip.

Prepare early:

- **Copy** for every page, at least a rough draft.
- **Photos** of products, team, location, finished work — high resolution.
- **A logo** in vector format (SVG or AI), not a screenshot.
- **Legal details** — registered name, address, tax number if it belongs in the footer.
- **Testimonials** with real names and the client's permission.

Design built on top of real content is almost always better than design filled with *lorem ipsum* and patched later.

## Step 6 — Get the technical SEO foundation right

SEO isn't something you bolt on at the end. Most of it is technical decisions that need to be correct from day one.

Minimum checklist:

- One `<h1>` per page, and it says something relevant.
- A unique, descriptive `title` on every page, around 60 characters.
- A unique `meta description`, around 150–160 characters.
- Human-readable URLs: `/services/web-development`, not `/page?id=42`.
- `sitemap.xml` and `robots.txt` in place.
- Every image carrying a meaningful `alt` attribute.
- Structured data (Schema.org) for your organisation, articles, or products.
- HTTPS enabled — no exceptions.

Page speed is also a ranking factor now. How to measure and fix it is covered in [our Core Web Vitals article](/blog/en/speed-up-your-website-core-web-vitals/).

## Step 7 — Test before you launch

Before you announce anything, run these checks:

1. **Every screen size** — 375px, 768px, 1024px, 1440px. Most traffic in this market is mobile.
2. **Every form** — send a test, confirm the email actually arrives (check spam).
3. **Every link** — internal and external, nothing returning a 404.
4. **Speed** — run PageSpeed Insights on mobile, not just desktop.
5. **The 404 page** — make sure it exists and sends people somewhere useful.

## Step 8 — Launch, then measure

Launch isn't the finish line. The first week live is when you finally get real data.

Install and check:

- **Google Search Console** — submit the sitemap, watch which pages get indexed.
- **Google Analytics** or a lighter alternative.
- **One headline number** tied to the goal from Step 1.

After that your job shifts from building to maintaining: publishing content, refreshing the portfolio, and fixing whatever the data says is broken.

## The mistakes we see most often

- **Letting the vendor own the domain.** When the relationship ends, your asset is hostage.
- **Having no admin access.** Ask for hosting, domain, and repository credentials on day one.
- **Chasing animation before the copy is right.** Motion never rescues a confusing message.
- **Starting a blog and stopping after three posts.** SEO pays out in month six, not week two.
- **Budgeting nothing for maintenance.** A website is a living asset, not a one-off purchase.

## How long does it take?

Realistic ranges from projects we've delivered:

- Company profile site (5–8 pages): **3–5 weeks**
- Site with a blog and structured service pages: **5–7 weeks**
- Web app with login, dashboard, and integrations: **8–16 weeks**

The biggest chunk of that time almost always goes to content, not development.
