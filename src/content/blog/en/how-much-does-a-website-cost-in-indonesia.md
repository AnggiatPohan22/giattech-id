The first question almost every prospect asks: "What does a website cost?" The honest answer is a wide range, because "a website" can mean five static pages or a reservation system with payments and an operations dashboard.

This article breaks that range into numbers you can actually budget against, and explains what genuinely drives the price.

## Price ranges by project type

The figures below are fair market rates in Indonesia in 2026 for work by an experienced freelancer or a small studio. Much cheaper usually means a re-skinned template; much more expensive usually means an agency with heavy overhead.

### Single landing page — IDR 3–10 million

One long page for a campaign, a single product, or validating an idea. Includes design, basic copy, a contact form, and responsive layout.

### Company profile site (5–8 pages) — IDR 8–25 million

Home, about, services, portfolio, contact, and a blog. This is the most common bracket for service businesses. Price climbs with the number of unique pages and how custom the design is.

### Website + CMS — IDR 15–40 million

The above, plus an admin panel so your team can add articles, products, or projects without a developer.

### Online store — IDR 20–70 million

Catalogue, cart, checkout, payment gateway integration, and order management. The range is wide because product count, variants, and shipping integrations dominate the effort.

### Custom web application — IDR 50–250 million+

Reservation systems, operations dashboards, customer portals, internal tools. Price is set by the number of user roles, the complexity of business rules, and third-party integrations.

## What actually drives the price up

Clients are often surprised that a fifth page is far cheaper than a first feature. Here are the cost drivers, largest first:

**1. Number of user roles.** An app with admin, staff, and customer roles needs three sets of testing, three access flows, and three permission sets. It's the single biggest multiplier.

**2. Third-party integrations.** Payment gateways, accounting systems, the WhatsApp Business API, an ERP. Each one brings its own documentation, failure cases, and error handling.

**3. Complicated business rules.** "10% off except Fridays, except returning customers, except when another voucher applies" — sentences like that are expensive to encode and to test.

**4. Fully custom design.** Designing from scratch with several revision rounds is a lot more work than adapting an existing design system.

**5. Data migration.** Moving thousands of products or customers off an old system almost always takes longer than anyone estimates.

**6. Multi-language requirements.** Doubles the content, the review cycle, and the testing.

Conversely, these are **not** as expensive as people assume: adding more similar static pages, changing colours and fonts, or adding a field to an existing form.

## The recurring costs people forget

The build price is only the first part. Budget annually for:

| Item | Estimated cost per year |
| --- | --- |
| `.com` domain | IDR 150,000 – 250,000 |
| `.co.id` domain | IDR 250,000 – 500,000 |
| Shared hosting | IDR 500,000 – 2,500,000 |
| Managed VPS | IDR 3,000,000 – 15,000,000 |
| SSL certificate | IDR 0 (Let's Encrypt) – 2,000,000 |
| Business email | IDR 700,000 – 1,500,000 per user |
| Maintenance & updates | 10–20% of build cost |

That last line is the one most often skipped. An unmaintained website ages badly: stale dependencies, security holes, and content nobody trusts.

## Pricing models: which fits you

### Fixed price

Clear scope, fixed number, fixed schedule. Good when you know exactly what you need. The risk: mid-project changes become negotiations.

### Time and materials

Pay by the hour or day. Good for exploratory work or ongoing maintenance. Requires trust and clean reporting.

### Monthly retainer

A set number of hours each month for fixes, updates, and small additions. This is usually the right shape after launch.

## Signs a quote is too cheap

An offer far below market usually hides one of these:

- **A re-skinned template** with your logo and no structural adaptation.
- **No revisions** after handover.
- **Domain and hosting registered to the vendor**, so you can't move.
- **No access to the source code.**
- **No support** after the final payment.
- **Speed and basic SEO excluded**, then sold back to you as an add-on.

Cheap isn't automatically bad — but ask about all six before signing anything. The full checklist lives in [choosing a web development vendor](/blog/en/checklist-for-choosing-a-web-development-vendor/).

## How to build a realistic budget

1. **Write down the business goal**, not a feature list. "Cut booking phone calls by 50%" is more useful than "have a booking form".
2. **Separate must-have from later.** Phase one should be enough to start earning.
3. **Hold 15–20% in reserve.** Something always surfaces mid-project.
4. **Budget first-year maintenance** up front, not after something breaks.
5. **Ask for a breakdown, not one number.** A good quote itemises the work.

## What a good quote contains

- Specific scope, including what is **not** included
- A clear page or module count
- The number of revision rounds
- A schedule with milestone dates
- Who supplies content and images
- Ownership of source code, domain, and assets
- A warranty period after launch
- Payment terms

## What's the return?

A website is an investment, so run the numbers like one. If your average client is worth IDR 15 million and the site brings three extra clients a year, a IDR 25 million site pays for itself inside year one.
